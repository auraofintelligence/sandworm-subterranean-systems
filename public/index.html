Build below. Give the surface back.

Better island services, less digging up our roads and a new local industry built around the very sand beneath our feet.

Imagine fixing a water leak without digging up the road

Most of us only think about pipes and cables when something goes wrong. Getting to a buried pipe or a cable on the seabed can be a big part of the repair job. Sandworm asks a different question: why not build a tunnel that lets robots reach those services from the inside?

A passage for pipes and cables

Think of a long, hollow passage roughly 1 to 1.5 metres across. Water, sewerage, electricity and communications could run through it, safely separated where needed.

Send in a robot

Instead of digging a fresh trench each time, robots could inspect equipment, repair faults or help install new services.

Make things from the sand

Test the sand that comes out and turn suitable material into building blocks, paving, glass and other useful products.

Sandworm proposes developing new autonomous tunnelling machines, materials, sensing and manufacturing technology specifically for wet silica and heavy mineral sands. We also want to explore utility tunnels beneath the bay floor. The tunnel size is an early research idea, not a final design.

Learn here, build here, keep the benefits here

Instead of buying all the technology and expertise from somewhere else, let's explore developing it on the islands. Local people could learn robotics, AI, design, materials research and digital manufacturing through ready SET Co-op and the Maker-Space Lab.

Mutual Futures asks how local workers and communities could own a share of the businesses and equipment doing the work. That includes the possibility of community future funds that reinvest returns locally.

Minjerribah, Mulgumpin, Bribie, South Stradbroke, Coochiemudlo, the Southern Moreton Bay Islands and other islands can each explore what works for them. Sharing technology and investment does not mean linking them for transport.

Why stop at today's technology?

Anyone who has dug a hole on the beach knows wet sand presents a very different challenge from solid rock. Existing engineering can teach us a lot, but it doesn't set the limits of what we can try. New ideas need to earn their place through practical tests.
