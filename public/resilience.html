Chapter 09 / 17 · Disaster resilience

Continuity above and below

Design for disruption, not a blanket claim that underground is safer.

Disaster use cases

Storm surge and tsunami

Surface access, inundation, trapped air, saltwater ingress and loss of power can make underground structures especially dangerous. Research service continuity separately from refuge.

Cyclones and flooding

Investigate protected and redundant equipment, emergency communications and service isolation. Compare against elevated infrastructure and mobile systems.

Extended outages

Explore distributed energy, spares, water treatment and communications with local operators. Keep essential functions independent of internet or blockchain availability.

Post-event recovery

Assess debris handling, temporary freight, repair logistics and inspection systems. Reopen assets only after appropriate safety assessment.

Life safety is a separate programme

Underground emergency accommodation is not assumed feasible or preferable. Public evacuation follows official emergency advice, including moving to higher ground where directed. Any future occupied refuge would need hazard-specific analysis, safe access, ventilation, fire protection, independent egress, water exclusion, emergency power and competent authorities' approval. No underground route is currently designated as a public shelter.

Evaluate public outcomes

Track time to restore services, number of people affected, availability of potable water and communication, ecological damage, maintenance cost, accessibility and dependency failures. Compare against surface alternatives before selecting an underground option.
