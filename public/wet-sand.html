Chapter 04 / 17 · Wet-sand engineering

How do you dig a tunnel in wet sand?

Think of digging a hole on the beach. Now imagine inventing machinery that can work safely beneath it, without draining or disturbing the island's water.

Wet sand isn't rock

Anyone who has dug a hole on the beach has seen the sides slump or the water creep in. A tunnel poses a much bigger version of that challenge. Sandworm is about learning to work specifically with wet silica and heavy mineral sands.

The island's drinking water comes first

Minjerribah relies on groundwater. Water sits in different layers of sand, including isolated freshwater lenses. A tunnel must not become a drain for the island's water supply or a path for saltwater to enter it. Begin with contained experiments, not live aquifers.

Seqwater water security programme ↗

Build around the maintenance robot

Start with a passage roughly 1 to 1.5 metres across. Work out how much space the lining, pipes, cables and robot need. Can it reach a valve, remove a broken part and get back out if it breaks down?

Research seals, strength, corrosion, water pressure, wear, energy use and ways to isolate different services. The Gumpi Town Tunnel Loop will need a separate design for passengers and goods.

Don't start with someone else's machine and work backwards

Develop autonomous digging equipment, materials and repair technology around the actual sand and water. Existing methods are useful comparisons. Measure energy used, water kept out, wear, durability and speed. Even an unsuccessful test teaches us something.
